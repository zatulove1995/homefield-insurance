What home insurance leads actually cost.
Per lead is the wrong number. Here's the right one.

The cost of a home insurance lead in 2026 is a published range, not a single price: shared web leads run $15–$35 (Insurance Leads Guide) or $8–$20 (InsureLeads), exclusive ones $25–$75 or $18–$40, live transfers $40–$100 or $25–$55, aged leads under $3 (Aged Lead Store) to $3–$10. What matters is the cost per policy written.

Published ranges

The short answer

  • Shared web lead: about $10–$45 (all lines); $15–$35 for homeowners — ActiveProspect, 2026-05-15; Insurance Leads Guide, 2026.
  • Exclusive web lead: about $45–$120 (all lines); $25–$75 for homeowners, and one vendor lists home at $45 flat — ActiveProspect; Insurance Leads Guide; InsureLeads, 2026-03-04.
  • Live transfer: about $80–$200+ (all lines); $40–$100 for homeowners — ActiveProspect; Insurance Leads Guide.
  • Aged leads: about $0.50–$15 (all lines — ActiveProspect, 2026-05-15); for home, $0.75–$3.00 (Aged Lead Store, 2025-06-09), $0.30–$2.50 (InsureLeads per-lead guide, 2026-03-04) or $3–$10 (InsureLeads home guide, 2026-03-05) — three sources, three ranges, listed separately on purpose.

All ranges are third-party published figures accessed 2026-09-14; two of the largest marketplaces (EverQuote, QuoteWizard) publish no per-lead price. Homefield's rate is not on this page — see "How Homefield bills" below for why.

Home specifically

How much do home insurance leads cost?

Home sits at the bottom of every band because the commission on a home policy is smaller than on life, Medicare or health: Insurance Leads Guide's 2026 homeowners pricing puts shared web leads at $15–$35, exclusive web at $25–$75, live transfers at $40–$100, telemarketed at $20–$45 and direct-mail-generated at $15–$35; InsureLeads' 2026 home-specific guide runs lower still — shared $8–$20, exclusive $18–$40, live transfer $25–$55, aged $3–$10, new-homebuyer $30–$60 — and its cross-line table lists home exclusive web at $45, live transfer at $100 and aged at $0.30–$2.50. Aged Lead Store's own pricing puts aged home leads at $0.75–$3.00.

None of these is a Homefield figure; the renewal-timed home insurance lead is described on its own page.

What moves a home lead's price

  • Filters — home value, property type, coverage status

Price drivers

What moves the price

DRIVER 01

Line of business

Life and Medicare leads cost several times a home lead because the commission is larger. Auto lead pricing context for agencies that write home and auto is on the home and auto page.

DRIVER 02

Exclusivity and buyer count

An exclusive lead costs more because you aren't racing; EverQuote's 2020 article put the gap at about $4–5 per lead.

DRIVER 03

Filters

Home value, ZIP, property type each add to a base price. QuoteWizard describes its price as "the price of the lead plus the cost of any filters we apply."

DRIVER 04

Freshness

Real-time vs aged.

DRIVER 05

Source and consent documentation

Leads with documented consent cost more (ActiveProspect, 2026-05-15).

DRIVER 06

Delivery format

A routed call costs several times a web form.

The number that matters

The number that decides it: cost per acquired policy

Cost per lead is what vendors quote because it's the number they control. Cost per written policy is the number that decides whether a channel works, and it moves with close rate more than with price. InsureLeads' own arithmetic makes the point (2026-03-04): a $50 lead that closes at 20% costs $250 per policy; a $10 lead that closes at 2% costs $500 per policy. The cheaper lead is twice as expensive.

Purchased-lead economics get worse the more buyers a lead has. On a shared marketplace you pay whether or not you win the lead — and the vendors' own advice is a speed race: in a 2015 article for agents, EverQuote tells them to call within one minute; SmartFinancial says the first agent to make contact often has the strongest opportunity. In a Harvard Business Review audit of 2,241 U.S. companies, 23% never responded to a web lead and the average response among those that did was 42 hours; firms responding within an hour were about seven times as likely to qualify the lead (HBR, March 2011). If your team can't dial inside a minute, a shared lead's effective price is higher than its sticker.

A Homefield lead is a homeowner who wrote back to your agency and no one else, so there is no race and no second buyer. Businesses using Homefield have reported 20–25% close rates with fast follow-up. At those rates a qualified lead turns into a customer for roughly four to five times its per-lead rate — whatever that rate is for your industry. That is an illustration of the arithmetic, not a promise; results depend on your follow-up. The cross-channel comparison with direct mail is on the compare page.

Two meanings of one word

“Exclusive” on a marketplace often isn't exclusive

Two different things wear the word. On a marketplace, an exclusive lead is a form fill that isn't resold — EverQuote's FAQ says an exclusive lead is "provided to one agent only," while its shared leads go to "a maximum of 3 agents" and QuoteWizard's to "a maximum of four" (their own pages, as of September 2026). MediaAlpha sells each shared lead "to no more than four buyers." HometownQuotes says up to four, and SmartFinancial "often between two and four." None of those vendors publishes a per-lead dollar price.

Territorial exclusivity — one agency per ZIP, locked, with the lead a reply to your own email — is a different product, and it's the one Homefield sells. The distinction matters for cost because the marketplace lead's price doesn't include what you spend to win the race.

Beyond the per-lead price

Deposits, minimums and contracts — the hidden line items

  • Prepaid deposits. Some vendors fund an account before the first lead: MediaAlpha's minimum deposit is $250 (its FAQ); Datalot's master agreement has agents pre-pay by card and bid per call; EverQuote's card deposit is set at sign-up and auto-recharges (its FAQ, amount not published); QuoteWizard's is likewise set at sign-up (its FAQ). EverQuote and MediaAlpha both state there is no minimum spend; what they require is a funded account. All as of September 2026.
  • Contracts. EverQuote, SmartFinancial, HometownQuotes and MediaAlpha publish "no contract" or "no long-term contract." Datalot's master agreement runs one year with automatic renewal, terminable on 72 hours' notice (app.datalot.com/legal).
  • Returns. Credit-only, time-boxed and often capped: EverQuote 30 days, up to 20% of a month's leads; MediaAlpha 7 days (72-hour returns auto-approved), 20% cap; SmartFinancial 10 days, credit never cash; HometownQuotes and ZipQuote 7 days.
  • Homefield. No deposit, no platform fee, no contract; a one-time onboarding fee; weekly invoices in arrears; 7-day dispute window; 30-day billing-error window refunded in full (Refund Policy).

Our model

How Homefield bills — and why there's no per-lead number on this page

Homefield bills per qualified lead: a homeowner in your territory who replied asking for a quote, asking what you'd charge, or asking to be contacted (what a Homefield insurance lead is). The rate is set by industry — a home insurance lead, a roofing lead and an off-market seller lead are worth different amounts, so they're priced differently — and it's quoted to you in writing on the call, after your territory is checked live. It steps down when you pass 50 qualified leads in a calendar month and again when you pass 299. Everything else about the model is public:

No dollars, just R

The arithmetic, with your rate as R

Call your industry's per-lead rate R. A hundred qualified replies in a month cost 100 × R. At a 20% close rate, 20 of 100 replies become policies, so a written policy costs about 5 × R; at 25%, about 4 × R — an illustration of the arithmetic at the close rates participating businesses have reported, not an expectation. The eighty-odd homeowners who didn't close this month are still your leads, with full follow-up rights, at next year's renewal. The variable that moves the number most is how fast the reply gets answered.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions about what insurance leads cost

How much do insurance leads cost in 2026?

Published third-party ranges run about $10–$45 for a shared web lead, $45–$120 for an exclusive web lead, $80–$200 or more for a live transfer and $0.50–$15 for aged leads (ActiveProspect, updated 2026-05-15); home insurance sits at the low end of each band. The largest marketplaces don't publish per-lead prices. Homefield's rate is set by industry and quoted in writing on the call.

How much do home insurance leads cost?

By source, 2026: Insurance Leads Guide — shared web $15–$35, exclusive web $25–$75, live transfer $40–$100; InsureLeads' home guide (2026-03-05) — shared $8–$20, exclusive $18–$40, live transfer $25–$55, aged $3–$10; InsureLeads' cross-line guide (2026-03-04) — exclusive home $45, live transfer $100, aged $0.30–$2.50; Aged Lead Store (2025-06-09) — aged home $0.75–$3.00. Those are prices for form fills, calls and files, each range one source's own. A Homefield home insurance lead is a homeowner's written reply, billed per qualified reply at a rate quoted on the call.

Are exclusive insurance leads worth the premium over shared?

Usually, if the alternative is racing up to two or three other agencies to the phone (EverQuote caps a shared lead at three agents in total, QuoteWizard and MediaAlpha at four — their own pages, as of September 2026): the price of a shared lead doesn't include the leads you pay for and don't win. InsureLeads' example — a $50 lead at 20% close costs $250 per policy, a $10 lead at 2% costs $500 — is the arithmetic. Whether a specific vendor's "exclusive" lead is worth its price depends on your close rate on it, which is why we'd suggest tracking cost per written policy for 60 days rather than trusting anyone's table, including ours.

What is a good cost per acquired policy for a home insurance lead?

There's no published benchmark for home specifically; the third-party figures in circulation are for life ($2,000–$3,000 per client at 2–3% close, per ActiveProspect and EliteRT) and are not comparable. The honest method is your own: leads bought × price paid ÷ policies written, tracked in your CRM for two months, channel by channel. Homefield's arithmetic is a qualified lead turning into a customer for roughly four to five times its per-lead rate at the 20–25% close rates businesses have reported — illustrative, not a promise.

What is the difference between a disputed lead and a billing error?

A disputed lead is one that was billed and, you believe, shouldn't have qualified — wrong territory, no real interest, a misread reply; flag it within 7 days of delivery and we review the thread against the definition. A billing error is arithmetic — a lead invoiced twice, a rate applied wrongly, a step-down missed; report it within 30 days and it is refunded in full. Neither covers the things that are never billed in the first place (bounces, auto-replies, unsubscribes, out-of-territory, duplicates, wrong contacts, hostile replies, declines), because those never reach the invoice.

What does Homefield charge per insurance lead?

A per-lead rate set by industry and quoted to you in writing on the call, after your ZIPs are checked live — it isn't printed here because it's set per industry, not per visitor. It steps down above 50 and above 299 qualified leads in a calendar month. A one-time onboarding fee applies; there's no platform fee, no deposit and no contract.

Is buying insurance leads worth it for a small agency?

It depends on one number: what a written policy costs you through the channel, including the leads you don't win and the time you spend dialing. Small agencies without a same-day follow-up habit tend to lose the speed race on shared leads. A reply-based channel changes the math because the homeowner has already asked you a question — but it takes about 21 days to the first lead and needs 100+ ZIPs of real population. Run both for 60 days and let your CRM decide.
